# Story 11.4 — Calibration instrumentation on 10.4

**Epic:** 11 — Migration readiness
**Status:** Retired 2026-08-25 — its purpose does not exist. Not superseded, not
blocked, not deferred. See "Why this is retired" below before reopening it.

## Why this is retired

This story exists to produce four numbers — endpoint hours, screen hours,
one-time primitive cost, post-gate defects — that would make a completion date
for Epic 10 defensible rather than a confident guess.

**Two things ended it, and the second is the one that matters.**

Its measurement target shipped un-instrumented. 10.4 landed without recording any
of the four, so the story could not run as written. That alone would have argued
for a rewrite pointing at 10.5 or 10.6.

Then the premise was checked with Sam on 2026-08-25 and the answer was: **there
is no timeline.** Nobody is owed a date for this project. So the four numbers
have no consumer, and Epic 11's rule that "no completion date is committed before
those four numbers exist" was guarding a promise nobody makes.

**Why it survived this long.** It reads as discipline. A rule refusing to commit
a date without measurement is the kind of thing that looks obviously correct and
gets waved through, and its cost — instrumenting a feature story, then running a
second story to collect the results — is paid before anyone asks who the date is
for. It is the same failure as Epic 15's "live bug" and story 5.11's "months in
production", in a more flattering costume: a claim nobody checked because
checking it felt unnecessary.

**What is worth keeping, and it is much smaller than this story.** Whether the
migration is going faster or slower *than it was going* is a useful steering
signal. That needs a rough note of how long each migration story took, kept as a
trend. It does not need four measured quantities, an instrumented feature story,
or this document. If someone wants the trend, record it in the migration stories
themselves.

**Reopen this only if the premise changes.** If a date is ever owed to anyone —
a client commitment, an external dependency, a hire — then the reasoning in the
original story below is sound and worth reviving. Retiring it is a statement
about the absence of a consumer, not a judgement that the method was wrong.

## Story

As whoever has to give a date for Epic 10, I want the first real feature story
to produce measurements rather than a feeling, so that the six after it can be
estimated from something.

## Context

Epic 10 has seven stories. One of them is done, two are in progress, and four
are drafts whose size nobody has measured. Any date given today is a guess
dressed as a plan.

10.4 is the right story to instrument because it is the first one that ships a
real feature end to end: endpoints, a typed service, components, widgets, tests,
and the deletion of the Next pages it replaces. Everything 10.5, 10.6 and 10.7
do is that same shape at different scale. If 10.4's numbers are recorded, the
rest become arithmetic on a known unit rather than invention.

Instrumenting after the fact does not work. Nobody reconstructs how long the
endpoint layer took once the story is merged, and the interesting number is not
total elapsed time but the split between its parts.

## Acceptance criteria

1. 10.4 records, per part: endpoints, service layer, components, widgets, tests,
   Next deletion. Effort and calendar, kept separately.
2. It records counts that predict the others: endpoints shipped, actions
   replaced, components created, Next files deleted, lines added and removed on
   each side.
3. It records what was not anticipated, in enough detail to be a warning rather
   than a note.
4. A per-unit figure comes out of it: cost per endpoint and cost per screen,
   stated with its own error bars.
5. 10.5, 10.6 and 10.7 carry estimates derived from those figures, with the
   derivation shown, not just the conclusion.
6. The record lives in the repo, next to the story, not in a chat.

## Tasks

- [ ] Decide the measurement shape before 10.4 starts, and put the empty table
      in the story doc
- [ ] Record during the work, not after
- [ ] Compute per-endpoint and per-screen figures with their spread
- [ ] Apply them to 10.5, 10.6 and 10.7 using each story's own inventory from
      11.5
- [ ] Write down where the estimate is least trustworthy and why

## Dev notes, what to measure and what to ignore

Wall-clock elapsed time is the least useful number available and the easiest to
collect. It folds in interruptions, review latency and whether anyone was
working that afternoon. Effort split by part is what transfers between stories.

Counts are the honest predictor here because the stories genuinely are the same
work repeated: 10.5 is four screens and a widget set, 10.6 is two surfaces
sharing a registry, 10.7 is six small modules and a deletion. Cost per endpoint
and cost per screen carry across all three.

## Dev notes, the failure mode this story exists to prevent

The plausible bad outcome is not a wrong estimate; it is a confident one. Epic
11 exists because 11.1 found a gate that had never run while its stories were
being marked verified. The equivalent failure here is a Gantt chart built on
one story's remembered impression. An estimate that states its own spread is
more useful than a single number, and much harder to be quietly wrong with.
